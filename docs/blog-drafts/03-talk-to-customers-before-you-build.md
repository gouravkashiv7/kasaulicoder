# Talk to Your Customers Before You Write a Line of Code

Here is a mistake we see often. A founder has a good idea. They spend three months and a lot of money building it. They launch. Almost nobody uses it.

The code was fine. The idea was fine. The problem was that nobody had asked the people it was built for.

You can avoid this. You do not need a research team or a big budget. You need about ten honest conversations and the right five questions. This guide shows you how.

## Why talk first?

Software is expensive to change once it is built. A conversation is not.

When you talk to customers first, you learn three things before you spend a rupee on development:

- **Is the problem real?** Does it actually hurt, and how often?
- **Who has it?** Not "everyone". A real person with a real job.
- **How do they cope today?** Spreadsheets, WhatsApp, paper, or nothing at all. That tells you what you are really competing with.

Product people call this *customer discovery*. Steve Blank helped make the idea popular, and Rob Fitzpatrick wrote a short book about the conversations themselves, called *The Mom Test*. The name comes from a simple idea: even your mother should not be able to lie to you about your idea, if you ask the right way.

## Real problem or nice idea?

People say kind things about ideas. "That sounds great!" "I would definitely use that." These feel good, and they tell you almost nothing. Saying yes costs people nothing.

A **real problem** looks different. The person can tell you about the last time it happened. They have already tried to fix it. It costs them time, money or customers. They will give you something real, such as their time, an introduction, or a deposit.

> *Imagine a homestay owner in a hill town who takes every booking over WhatsApp. If you ask, "Would you use a booking app?", they will probably say "sure". If you ask, "Tell me about the last time you got a double booking," you may hear about a stressful weekend, an angry guest and a refund. The second answer is the one worth building on.*

## Step 1: Find ten people (even if you know nobody)

Start with ten. We use that number as a rule of thumb, not a scientific fact. Keep going until you hear the same stories again and again. When you stop hearing anything new, you have learned what you can.

Talk to people who have the problem, not to friends who want to be nice. Where to look:

- **Your own circle's circle.** Ask friends, "Who do you know who runs a clinic / a shop / a school?" Then ask that person.
- **Local groups.** Business WhatsApp groups, trade associations, market committees, co-working spaces, college alumni networks.
- **Online communities** where your customers already talk about their work.
- **Walk in.** For local businesses, a short visit often works better than a message.

Be honest about who you are and what you want. Say you are researching a problem, not selling anything.

## Step 2: Open the conversation

People are busy. Make it easy to say yes. Here is a short script you can adapt:

> "Hi, I'm [name]. I'm trying to understand how [people like you] handle [problem area]. I'm not selling anything. Could I ask you a few questions for about 20 minutes? I'd love to learn how it works for you today."

At the start of the call:

- Say how long it will take, and stick to it.
- Ask if you can take notes. If you want to record, ask first, and respect a no.
- Tell them there are no wrong answers.

## Step 3: Ask these five questions

You do not need forty questions. Five good ones, followed by "tell me more", will take you most of the way.

| # | Question | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **"Tell me about the last time you [dealt with this problem]."** | Whether it really happens, and the real story, not an opinion |
| 2 | **"What did you do about it?"** | What they already tried, and how much they care |
| 3 | **"What did it cost you?"** (time, money, customers, stress) | How big the problem is |
| 4 | **"What do you use today, and what do you dislike about it?"** | Your real competition, and the gaps |
| 5 | **"Who else should I talk to? Is there anything I should have asked?"** | Your next interviews, and what you missed |

### Three rules that make these questions work

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.** Do not pitch. The moment you describe your idea, people start talking about it instead of about their problem.
2. **Ask about specific times in the past, not the future.** "What did you do last time?" is a fact. "Would you use this?" is a guess.
3. **Talk less, listen more.** Silence is fine. Let them fill it. If they say something interesting, ask "Can you tell me more about that?"

### Avoid these questions

| Instead of | Ask |
| --- | --- |
| "Would you use an app that does X?" | "How do you handle X today?" |
| "Would you pay ₹500 a month for this?" | "How much does this problem cost you now?" |
| "Do you think this is a good idea?" | "Tell me about the last time this went wrong." |
| "What features do you want?" | "What is the hardest part of doing this today?" |

## Step 4: Tell polite answers from real signals

After each conversation, ask yourself: did they give me a compliment, or did they give me a commitment?

| Polite answer (weak) | Real signal (strong) |
| --- | --- |
| "That sounds great!" | They describe a specific, recent time the problem hurt |
| "I'd definitely use that." | They already spend time or money on a workaround |
| "You should add feature X." | You ask why, and the underlying need is clear |
| "Keep me posted." | They agree to a second meeting, introduce you to someone, share their real data, or offer to pay for an early version |

Strong signals are not about being nice. They are about the person spending something: their time, their reputation, or their money.

## Step 5: Write it down and decide

Do this within an hour of each conversation, while you still remember:

- **Their situation:** who they are, in one line.
- **The story:** the last time the problem happened.
- **What they tried:** and what it cost them.
- **Their words:** exact phrases. You will use them later in your product and your marketing.
- **Commitments:** any real follow-up they agreed to.

After about ten conversations, read your notes in one go. Write the problem in one sentence, in your customers' words. Then choose one of three paths:

1. **Go.** Most people described the same problem, and it clearly hurts. Build the smallest version that solves it.
2. **Narrow.** The problem is real, but only for one type of person. Focus on them.
3. **Stop or change.** People were polite, but nobody had the problem in a way that costs them. This is a good result. You just saved months of work and a lot of money.

## Be kind to the people who help you

The people you talk to are giving you time and trust. Treat them well:

- Thank them, and tell them what you learned if they ask.
- Take only the notes you need, and keep them private.
- Do not use a research chat to sneak in a sales pitch.
- If they say they are busy or want to stop, stop.

This is not only good manners. People who feel respected tell you the truth, and they tell their friends.

## Copy this one-page checklist

**Before**
- [ ] I wrote down the problem I think exists, in one sentence.
- [ ] I listed 10 people who might have it (not friends who will be polite).
- [ ] I have a short opening message that says I am not selling.

**During**
- [ ] I said how long it would take and asked permission to take notes.
- [ ] I asked about the last time, not about the future.
- [ ] I asked the five questions: last time, what they did, what it cost, what they use now, who else to ask.
- [ ] I did not pitch my idea.
- [ ] I talked less than they did.

**After**
- [ ] I wrote notes within one hour, using their words.
- [ ] I marked each conversation: compliment or commitment.
- [ ] After about 10 conversations, I wrote the problem again, in their words.
- [ ] I chose: go, narrow, or stop.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many customers should I talk to before building?

There is no magic number, and sources disagree. We suggest starting with ten, then continuing until you keep hearing the same stories. If you still hear new things after ten, talk to more people.

### What if I don't know anyone in the industry?

Ask friends for introductions, join local business groups and online communities, or visit businesses in person. Say clearly that you are researching, not selling. Most people are happy to talk about their work.

### Should I talk to friends and family?

They are good for practice and for introductions. They are poor for validation, because they want to be kind. Talk to strangers who have the problem.

### Can I use a survey instead?

Surveys help you count things you already understand. They are poor at discovering things you do not know yet. Start with conversations, then use a survey later to check what you found.

### What if people say they would pay?

That is nice to hear, but it is not proof. Ask for something small and real: a second meeting, an introduction, or a deposit for an early version. What they do matters more than what they say.

### Do I need to do this if I am hiring a software company?

Yes, and a good team will want to help. The more you know about your customers' problem before the project starts, the less you spend on changes later.

## The takeaway

Ten honest conversations cost you a few evenings. Building the wrong product costs you months. Ask about their life, not your idea. Listen for real stories and real commitments, not compliments.
