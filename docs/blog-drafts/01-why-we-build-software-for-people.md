# Why We Build Software for People, Not Just for Clients

A client can sign off on a project that is finished, on time, and still no good to the people who have to use it.

We have seen how that happens, and we do not want to build that kind of software. This post explains what we do instead, in plain words, so you can judge whether we are the right team for you.

## What "customer-centric" actually means

You will see the phrase *customer-centric software development* on almost every agency website. It sounds good, and it is easy to claim. Most of the pages we read say the same thing: involve the customer. Few show what that looks like on a Tuesday afternoon in a real project.

So here is our short version:

> **We start with the problem of the person who will use the software. Then we build the smallest thing that solves it, show it to them early, and keep listening after launch.**

That is it. No magic. The hard part is doing it every time, including when it is inconvenient.

## Two people, one project

Every software project has at least two groups of people:

- **The client.** The person who hires us and pays the bill.
- **The users.** The people who sit in front of the screen every day. Sometimes they are the client's staff. Sometimes they are the client's customers.

Often these two groups want different things. Here is a simple, **imagined** example.

> *Imagine a small clinic that asks for an online booking system. The owner wants fewer phone calls. The patients, many of them older, want to book without creating an account and a password. If we only listen to the owner, we may build a system that looks tidy but that half the patients cannot use. The phone keeps ringing, and the clinic has paid for something that does not help.*

A good project works for both groups. If we have to choose, we ask a simple question: **who will be harmed if we get this wrong?** Usually, it is the person with the least say in the room.

## Our four principles

### 1. Start with the problem, not the technology

We do not begin with "which framework should we use?" We begin with "what is making your day harder?" The technology comes after we understand the answer. Sometimes the right answer is not custom software at all. A spreadsheet and two connected tools may be enough, and we will say so.

### 2. Be honest about scope, cost and limits

We scope each project individually and send a proposal. We do not sell fixed packages that hide what you are really getting. We write down what is in and what is out. If something will not work, or will cost more than it is worth, you will hear it from us early.

We also do not promise results nobody can control. For example, no honest team can promise you the number one spot on Google.

### 3. Protect the people who trust the product

Users hand software their phone numbers, health details, and money. That is a trust. We collect only what a feature needs, explain in plain words why, and keep it safe. We do not use tricks to make people click, stay, or pay by accident.

We also think about who might be left out: people on slow connections, older phones, or with poor eyesight. Software that only works for some people is half-finished.

### 4. A win for you, your customers and us

The best projects leave everyone better off. You get a product that works. Your customers get something useful. We get a client who recommends us. If a project helps only one of those three at the expense of the others, it will not last.

That is why we aim to leave you in control. Your code and your data are yours, and we document what we build so another team could pick it up. We do not want to keep you because you are stuck. We want you to stay because we are useful.

## What changes in practice

Principles are cheap. What matters is what we do differently. Here is how they show up at each stage.

| Stage | A "build what the spec says" team | How we try to work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Before the quote** | Sends a price from a feature list | Asks about your users and the problem first, then writes a proposal for your project |
| **Planning** | Builds everything on the list | Picks the one workflow that matters most and builds that first |
| **Building** | Shows you the finished product at the end | Shows working pieces along the way so you and your users can react early |
| **Launch** | Hands over the files and moves on | Checks that real people can use it, and fixes what they struggle with |
| **After launch** | Charges for every change, or disappears | Offers ongoing support, and keeps you free to leave |

None of this is exotic. It just takes patience, and a willingness to hear "this is not working" while there is still time to fix it.

## What we say no to

You can learn a lot about a team from what it refuses to do. We will politely decline, or push back on, work like this:

- **Tricks that mislead users.** Fake countdown timers, hidden fees, cancel buttons that are impossible to find.
- **Collecting data "just in case."** If we cannot explain why a feature needs it, we do not collect it.
- **Promises nobody can keep.** Guaranteed rankings, guaranteed revenue, guaranteed overnight growth.
- **Features that look good in a demo but do not help real users.**
- **Anything meant to harm or cheat the people who use it.**

Saying no can cost us a project. We would rather lose a project than build something we would not want our own families to use.

## A quick checklist for choosing any software team

You do not have to hire us to use this. Ask these questions of any team you are thinking of working with:

1. Did they ask about the people who will use the software, or only about features?
2. Can they explain, in words you understand, why they recommend one approach over another?
3. Will you see working pieces before the end, or only a final reveal?
4. Does the proposal say what is *not* included?
5. Who owns the code and the data when the project ends?
6. What will they refuse to build?
7. What happens after launch, and what will it cost?

A team that answers all seven clearly and calmly is a good sign.

## A note on professional standards

We are not the first people to think this way. The Association for Computing Machinery (ACM), a large professional body for people who work in computing, publishes a Code of Ethics. It begins with the idea that people who build software should contribute to society and to human well-being. We agree. We do not follow the code perfectly, and we will not claim that we do. We use it as a standard to measure ourselves against.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is customer-centric software development?

It means building software around the real needs of the people who will use it, and asking them for feedback during the project, not only at the start and the end.

### Is it more expensive?

Not usually. Listening early costs far less than rebuilding something nobody wanted. We do not claim a specific saving, because it depends on the project. We scope every project individually, so the best way to get a real number is to ask for a proposal.

### Does this slow down the project?

A little at the start, because we ask questions before we build. It tends to save time later, because fewer things need to be redone.

### What if my users and I disagree on what to build?

That is normal, and it is useful to find out early. We help you test the disagreement with a small prototype or a short conversation with a few real users, then decide together.

### Do you only work with local clients?

No. We are a small team based in Kasauli, in the hills of Himachal Pradesh, and we work with founders and businesses anywhere.

## The takeaway

Good software is not the software that matches the spec. It is the software that makes someone's day easier. We build for that person, we are honest with you about what is possible, and we try to leave everyone better off.
